## 1. Multisyllabic (compound) rhyme

Multisyllabic, compound and polysyllabic rhyme are the same idea: two or more syllables match, often across several short words (a "no way" / "snow day" type pair). In rap the match is mostly the VOWELS, not the consonants.

How to:
- Pick the end phrase first (2-3 syllables), then build the sentence backwards to land on it.
- Match the stressed vowels in order; let consonants drift. Say both aloud: if the vowel shape matches, it rhymes.
- Spend multis on the line you want remembered; use plain single rhymes around them so the ear can rest.

Example (invented, vowel run "ah-oh-ee"):
- "Sat in the car park, ordered a cold one"
- "Half-cut, Ma's calling, told her I'm sold on"
(vowel shape: car park/ordered/cold one against Ma's calling/told her/sold on)

## 2. Slant, near and assonance rhyme

Slant rhyme shares vowels but not the exact ending (crazy / baby type). It is the main way to rhyme without sounding like a nursery book, and it forgives Joe's accent (Reading/Bristol vowels: bath, lot, price).

How to:
- Rhyme the sound as Joe SAYS it (his accent), not the spelling. Check with `pron`.
- Keep the vowel and the stress position; change the last consonant ("lane / safe / change").
- Use true full rhyme for the one bar you want to hit like a gavel; slant for the rest.

Example (invented): "Flight was delayed, so I stayed / Had a pint, then another, then a tray of lasagne" (aim: "stayed / lasagne" is slant on the long A, lands via accent).

## 3. Internal rhyme and vowel carpets

Internal rhyme repeats the whole sound (vowel onward) inside the line. Assonance repeats just the vowel. A "vowel carpet" is one vowel sound scattered through 2-4 bars so the end rhymes feel inevitable. Tip from a songwriting guide: put the echoed syllable on a strong beat or the ear misses it; match open vowels with open vowels.

How to:
- Choose the vowel of the end-rhyme in bar 1 and plant it 2-3 times in the middle of bars 1-4.
- Put each plant on a stressed syllable (beats 1 and 3 or the snare).
- Rotate: change the carpet vowel every 4 bars so it never becomes a drone.

Example (invented, carpet on long "oh"): "Rome in the cold, so I stroll, no coat, no phone, / Nowhere to go, so I'm home by the show" (every stress is "oh").

## 4. Flow, pocket and syllable counting at ~142 BPM

Facts that are arithmetic: at 142 BPM a 4/4 bar lasts about 1.69 s. A bar has 16 sixteenth-note slots. Half-time means the snare lands on beat 3, so it FEELS like ~71 BPM while the hi-hats still run at 142; you get roughly the same 16 slots but each phrase is allowed to breathe over two "slow" beats.

Rules of thumb (all (unverified); vendor and blog figures, not measured standards): eighth-note flow is about 2 syllables per beat, sixteenth-note flow about 4; one syllable counter suggests 16-20 syllables per bar for dense verses. Treat as ranges to test, not targets.

How to:
- Syllable-count every bar (`syllmap`). Joe-style conversational bars usually sit around 10-14 syllables; the density bars (multis) can go 16+.
- Mark the stressed syllables and put them on the beats (1, 2, 3, 4), with the rhyme word on or just before beat 4 or the snare on 3.
- Leave a breath. A bar full to the brim twice in a row tires the ear; follow a dense bar with a sparse one.
- Read it over a metronome at 142, then again at half-time. If you trip, cut a syllable, do not rush it.

Example (invented, 12 syllables, stress in caps): "I'm ON a TRAIN to a TOWN with no NAME / and the CHEF's got a HAT and the BILL's a bit SAME" (stresses at 1,2,3,4 of each half-bar).

## 5. Cadence variety (pocket changes)

Cadence is where syllables land against the pulse and which words get stressed. Variety stops a long song going flat.

How to:
- Per 8 bars, change ONE thing: syllable density, where the rhyme lands (early, on-beat, late), or phrase length.
- Use a stop: a one-beat rest before the rhyme word makes a deadpan landing ("pause + punch").
- Use a lagging bar (late, behind the beat) for the flat, nonchalant report; use a rushed bar for the list or chaos bit.

Example (invented): dense six-syllable-in-a-beat run of place sounds, then a bar of 6 syllables only: "Booked it. Went. Was fine."

## 6. Punchline and wordplay structures

General guidance: a punchline is setup plus payoff; the payoff must connect to the setup. Joe's own definition differs: NO setup-punchline gags (CLAUDE.md #7). So use wordplay that is carried by the rhyme slot, not a gag that waits for a drum fill. From comedy_theory.md: the cashed-in word sits in the rhyme slot in nearly all wordplay lines.

Tools (use one per 4 bars at most):
- Cashed idiom: take a stock phrase literally and let the literal word be the rhyme. Invented: "Told the airline I'm flying solo, they gave me a seat by the wing."
- Homophone: a word that sounds like another (pour / Pavos type, bored though / Bordeaux type, Joe's own style). Let the accent do the work; mine pairs from Joe's own mouth, not a rhyming dictionary. Invented shape: "week off, felt weak" style pairs, said flat.
- Double meaning: one word, two readings, both true in context. The second meaning must be neat, or it is a groan. Invented: "Booked a room with a view, view was a skip."
- Understatement: report the huge thing in a small-errand voice. Invented: "Volcano went up, I'd already had lunch."
- Number ladder: repeat a unit with growing size so the last rung is absurd. Invented: "Twenty quid, fifty quid, then the exchange rate laughs / and I'm down to a dozen Euro coins and a map."
- Reversal on the rhyme word: let the listener expect the rhyme, then give a slant that is funnier. Use rarely.

The 'groan test': if you have to explain it, cut it.

## 7. Storytelling and pacing in verses

Sources: a common outline is intro, verse (set theme), hook, verse (develop), hook, bridge/contrast, final verse (resolve). Hooks of 2-4 bars. One tip: vary enjambment and caesura to control pace.

For a travel track (Joe's rule: no itinerary lists, no six places in eight bars):
- One place per 4-8 bars, one concrete image per place (a thing a mate would notice), one human (Mum, a bouncer, the lads).
- Open the verse with the quiet fact; end it on the biggest or flattest line.
- Keep time moving: use "then", "by Tuesday", "back for tea" as mile markers, not place names.
- Let the last 2 bars of a verse point at the hook.

Example (invented): "Landed in Lima, no luggage, no phrasebook / Got a lift from a lad who was wanted in Brooklyn. / Told Mum I was fine. She said 'which one'. / I said 'the one with the lorry'."

## 8. Hooks and bookends

- Hook = one strong, repeatable statement tied to the theme, shorter phrases than the verse, or slower; change the pace so it sounds different from the verses.
- Bookend: end the last verse with a line that echoes the hook's shape but changes one word. Do not add new ideas in the outro.
- Chant test: could four mates shout it on a night out? If yes, it is a hook.

Example (invented hook shell): "Back by Thursday, tell the missus, Thursday." -> final: "Back by Thursday, tell the missus. (Pause.) Friday."

## 9. Comedic British rap and spoken-word technique

All (unverified) general knowledge, not from fetched sources; the only supported observations are: Mike Skinner's reviews describe a conversational, pub-table, almost spoken-word delivery with ordinary detail (returning DVDs, gambling on football, watching soaps) made remarkable by being sung about, and an album-length narrative arc (one concept record about a lost shoebox of savings). Loyle Carner reviews: domestic detail, a letter-form opening, phone-call clips between songs. Slowthai: vivid daily-life storytelling, aggressive delivery.

Technique (describe, never quote), mapped to Joe:
- The Streets: flat spoken tone, rhythm follows speech not grid; the detail is the joke; time moves in conversation. Use for the deadpan errand voice. Write sentences you would say; fit the rhyme at the end.
- Dave: controlled, patient pacing; a single scene told in sequence; punch bars set with calm. Use the patience: slower is not weaker.
- Slowthai: shouted energy, scrappy lines, local detail, vulnerability in the chorus. Use contrast: one loud bar after three calm ones.
- Skinnyman: dense storytelling, real-life detail, little ornament. Use as a reminder that plain facts can hit hard.
- Lethal Bizzle, Pete & Bas: chantable hooks, crowd-call and response, mates-in-the-room. Use for the hook and the gang-vocal moments.
- Dizzee Rascal: jumpy, stop-start rhythm; slang as the music. Use the stop-start for the list/chaos bars.
- Loyle Carner: warm, domestic, mum/kitchen; story in small objects. Use for the Mum and missus bars.
- Little Simz: clear, steady delivery, confidence without shouting; personal storytelling and structure. Use for the confident quiet bar.

## 10. Keeping a real voice while polishing

From voice/profile.md: Joe keeps slang, accent puns, concrete money and objects, mates, rough grammar if it sounds like a bloke talking; he drops lines built to fit a tool.

How to:
- Polish order: (1) keep his key words and clause order, (2) adjust only the rhyme tail and syllable count, (3) add internal rhyme if it costs nothing.
- If a tweak makes the line sound like a lyric rather than speech, undo it.
- Read it as if telling a mate in the pub; if you would not say it, cut it.
- Log craft notes; voice wins ties.

Example (invented): raw: "Went home for week, got bored" -> polish: keep "Went home for week" (his) and fix only the end: "Went home for week, got bored, flew to Bordeaux." Done.

## 11. Cringe list: amateur and AI-rap mistakes, with fixes

(Sources: practitioner blogs; no rigorous study (unverified). "AI-sounding" is a craft problem to fix, not proof of authorship.)

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Stock imagery (moon, rain, scars, echoes, fading light, shattered dreams, road) | Interchangeable; could be any song | Swap for one concrete, local detail (a price, a brand, a name) |
| Emotion labels ("I feel lost") | Tells, does not show | Show the thing the narrator did |
| Rhyme drives meaning | Easy rhyme finishes the sentence before you decide the point | Write the plain sentence first, then find the rhyme |
| Maximum intensity every line | No restraint, no payoff | Alternate: flat, flat, big, flat |
| Brag-by-numbers (richest, best, king) | Generic swagger | Use nonchalance: the huge thing in an errand voice |
| Itinerary lists, six places in eight bars | Tour-guide, not funny (Joe's rule) | One place, one image, one human |
| Announced puns ("get it?") | Dead on arrival | Let it sit in the rhyme slot, say it flat |
| Obscure idioms nobody says | Not Joe's mouth | Use words a mate would say |
| Same rhyme sound too long | Drone | Change the carpet vowel every 4 bars |
| Even syllable count every bar | Robotic | Vary density and rhyme position |
| Over-explained jokes | Kills deadpan | Cut the second half |
| Weak ending words (to, and, the) | No landing | Rhyme on a stressed noun or verb |
| "Cause" and "yeah" filler | Padding | Cut or use as a deliberate beat |
| Real artist/film names without Joe's OK | Rule | Skip unless Joe says |

## 13. Bar checklist (one page)

For every bar, tick:
1. Would Joe say this to a mate, in his accent? (voice first)
2. One idea, one concrete thing (price, brand, name, object)?
3. Facts true or flagged (invented) or (persona)?
4. Syllables counted (`syllmap`); stresses on beats 1-4; rhyme word on the snare or beat 4?
5. Rhyme sound checked in Joe's accent (`pron`); vowel match at least on the stressed syllable?
6. Multi or internal rhyme only where it earns it; carpet vowel changed in the last 4 bars?
7. Wordplay (if any) sits in the rhyme slot, second meaning neat, said flat? Groan test passed?
8. Nonchalance: big thing reported in errand voice, no wink, no explanation?
9. Not an itinerary line; no scene-hop; one place per 4-8 bars?
10. Density varied vs the previous bar (dense then sparse)?
11. Ends on a stressed noun/verb, not a filler word?
12. No cliche image, no emotion label, no heavy swearing, no real artist/film name?
13. Read aloud at 142 and in half-time: any stumble means cut a syllable?
14. Final say: Joe's ear. Log craft note, do not overwrite his edit.
