# Comedy theory v1 (9 Oct 2026). Joe's definition leads; evidence and inference follow. No lyrics reproduced.

## JOE'S DEFINITION (authoritative; Joe knows the original best)
"There's actually not really any jokes at all, the comedy comes from the characters and wordplay."
1. CHARACTER / NONCHALANCE. The Dubai/doobies line is funny because it is so casual and nonchalant: going to a rich desert city to smoke weed and telling your wife you will be back shortly. The extraordinary is reported in a domestic, errand voice. No punchline; the attitude is the joke.
2. WORDPLAY. The halogen line ("lights out" cashed into a bulb) is funny because it is interesting and clever, but the rhyme scheme and flow are what sell it.
=> The unit of craft is the CHAIN (rhyme scheme + flow + internal rhyme), not the standalone gag bar. Bar types: C (character / nonchalance), W (wordplay), CW.

## Evidence that fits (small samples; unchecked)
- Reaction transcripts, new pass (n = 6, auto-captions, laughter inferred, line positions approximate, no comments): the firm laughs came from the performers' LOOK and AGE, not from wordplay. Wordplay lines (the network-not-social pun, the double-it run) earned admiration ("that's a bar"), not laughs. The best lyric line by count was a smuggling shipment described like next-day parcel delivery (a mundane register crashing into the criminal one, which is Joe's "nonchalance" in another form). Delivery earned admiration. Reactors paused or rewound 1-2 seconds after the key lines.
- Earlier pass (17 videos): ranked strengths were layered wordplay that rewards rewinds (about 15 sources), deadpan comic persona vs old-age visuals (about 16), flow and two voices (about 12). Named biggest laughs: the blue-pill gag, the parcel line, the hair line, the creeping cigarette price, the bribe.
- Web sources (secondary): "be yourself, turned up to eleven", total seriousness, "committed to the bit", domestic detail (pubs, baked potatoes, Sunday papers) beside gangster boasts, layered double meanings plus UK references. No craft interview exists; they say they write their own lyrics, keep it "clean-ish", and ask grandkids for slang.
- **Genius, line by line (done; full table in genius_line_classes.md; 42 of 42 annotations; classes are the agent's, partly its own judgement).** Of 81 unique items: C 17, CW 10, W 12, R (reference only) 27, F 3, X 12. So about a third carry the nonchalance (C+CW), about a quarter carry wordplay (W+CW), a third are accurate references (places, slang, brands, people), and about 15% are plain setup. Findings: (1) the C lines narrate an extraordinary act (killing, bribing, trafficking, smuggling) in an EVERYDAY BRITISH ADMIN OR CONSUMER REGISTER (parcel delivery, a pub chain, a schoolboy telling-off, cigarette prices, a wife's duty-free), usually as a 2-4 line run with a bland setup line and a bathetic closer on the rhyme beat; the narrator never reacts to danger and violence is euphemised as logistics or games. (2) The W lines cash a stock phrase into a literal commodity (lights out -> bulb, wrist slap -> banknote, tune/drum -> gun, boxed -> packaged) and the cashed-in word sits in the RHYME SLOT (CW lines 10 of 10, pure W 8 of 12, pure C only 5 of 17); the payoff often lands on the next line across the bar break. (3) The domestic third party is MENTIONED, not addressed: zero of 27 C lines are spoken to the wife; she appears through a message relayed via a mate. (4) The one classic gag is the cigarette-price tag; every other joke-shaped pair reduces to C or W. (5) No annotation or comment uses "funny", "joke" or "humor"; 28 of 42 annotations are plain glosses (translations, slang, places, brands). This supports Joe's definition. It also shows that references are a third of the song, so accurate local words and places are doing real work.

## Consequences for our song
- AUDIO ONLY: the original's visual half (old men, cane, hair, posture) is not available. The character must be carried by word choice and register: ordinary people in the narrator's life (Mum, landlord, mates), errand logic ("back for tea", "don't wait up"), British ordinary objects, flat sentences that just end.
- Persona: a 33-year-old from Reading by way of Bristol who treats world travel, volcanoes, fog-bound cliffs and festivals as errands. The nonchalance IS the swagger (so "swagger first" becomes "nonchalance first").
- Clone risk: avoid the original's frames ("tell her I'm out in X ... back shortly", "greeting to the boys in X", the plane/plain pun, "just touched down"). Use the attitude, not the template.
- Rubric changes needed: replace #12 "Joke density" with (a) Nonchalance density: share of bars that report a big, illicit or absurd thing in an errand voice with no punchline, and (b) Wordplay quality: a real second meaning, neat, carried by the rhyme. Drop "late landing" (a reaction-agent inference, not Joe's). Keep the groan test and the poster test (images should still look cool).
- The earlier "mundane comedown one bar in eight" rule is replaced: mundane domestic anchors are now part of the main voice, but itinerary logistics stay out.
- Brand names: the original uses everyday British brands and institutions. Our "no brand names" rule may cost recognition. Test in Suno; ask Joe.
